The Compute Commitment Register records which actors have committed computing capacity to which societal challenges, and on what evidence. Each record is one documented commitment, such as a budget line, an allocation award, or a securities filing — verified against its source and recorded as declared.

How it works

1
A body commits computing capacity in an official document, for example a budget line, an allocation record, or a filing.
2
The Observatory verifies the document against its source standards and records it once as a commitment.
3
The commitment is counted under every listed challenge its document names — most name one; some name several.

Coverage acknowledgement

The figures describe compute commitments the Observatory has located and verified against this standard, not the population of commitments that exist. Counts are floors. Coverage expands as instruments are found. A record's presence carries no information about its scale beyond its band; declared quantities are shown as published and are never compared or summed. The challenge list is a dated observation of four institutional sources and changes only at an epoch boundary.

Limits of this observation

As stated in the working note at publication; revised with each observation.

  1. Where the records come from. Nine of the eleven records rest on documents published by public allocation programmes. Governments state quantities in money or aggregate compute; corporations state them in press releases, which the register does not admit.
  2. Regional concentration. The challenge list rests on three European-authored institutions and the United Nations. No Asian, African or Latin American list qualified: those found are national research-priority lists, a different kind of document. Two of the four institutions survey perceived risk, which is why adverse outcomes of AI is a published challenge and AI-safety compute is counted under it. The qualifying definition is published so this concentration can be tested at each refresh.
  3. Energy supply. Named by two of the four institutions, one short of the threshold. Energy commitments are recorded and held; they enter the table when a third institution names energy.
  4. Privacy and data protection. No candidate covers data protection directly; the nearest, erosion of human rights, is below the threshold. Privacy research in the National Science Foundation's allocations is therefore recorded but not shown in the table.
  5. Purpose statements. 441 passages named no listed challenge and 174 stated no purpose at all. Under the assignment rule, work that names an AI method with a domain only as its application is not assigned, so part of the 441 reflects the strictness of the rule.
  6. Uneven publication. One actor's two records represent a programme with no published award list; another's single record represents a programme that has published 847 awards and supplies eight of the nineteen document–challenge pairs. Counts are floors of disclosure, not of activity.
  7. One attribution. At publication, one register record links to one corpus event.
  8. Pledges in press releases. Press releases, blogs and keynotes are not admitted, so a corporate or philanthropic pledge made only there cannot enter; the register holds no such record.
  9. Discovery circularity. Research models found the instruments. What they did not find is not in the register, so coverage reflects their reach.
